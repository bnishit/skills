# Product hypothesis pressure test

Turn an intuition into a claim that can survive a counterexample. Preserve the user's intended value; do not silently replace connection, variety, or shared enjoyment with more usage or shorter sessions.

## Work from the lived moment

Start with the specific person, entry point, rule, and trigger: what happened, what they wanted next, and what the product allowed. Separate:

- **Observed:** actions directly shown in evidence.
- **Reported:** what a person says happened, with the number of people or couples represented.
- **Hypothesis:** an explanation or expected effect, including inferred designer intent.
- **Unknown:** behavior or results not yet measured.

A recording of someone discussing their play confirms the discussion, not every described game outcome. An article's claims, the user's experience, and an assistant's interpretation are distinct sources. Use supplied evidence first; do not require Computer History for future uses of this skill.

In a live thinking conversation, ask one high-value question at a time. Challenge the answer before naming the insight. If the user asks for a finished assessment, use available evidence, state assumptions, and deliver it without forcing an interview.

## Walk through the next choice

Simulate the same moment with the rule and with a plausible alternative. Use a compact table: situation, what each person wants, next action, feeling at the stopping point, and possible next-day action. Treat simulated feelings as possibilities, not facts.

For a shared game, follow both partners through losing, winning, asking for another round, accepting or refusing, tying, and stopping. Check whether the rule gives a welcome ending or cuts off enjoyable play. For a two-round cap, consider 2–0, 1–1, and 0–2; do not assume a tie resolves the urge to play again.

Trace the proposed chain in plain words:

    Product rule → next choice → feeling → later action → user value

Challenge the weakest link. A cap on one game does not cap time in the app. Moving to another game is different from leaving. An invitation tomorrow is different from both partners wanting to return.

## Try to break the explanation

Choose the counterexample most likely to change the decision. For daily couple play, useful probes include:

- One partner enjoys the game more. Does the next invitation feel welcome or like a duty?
- Partners win at different games. Does variety let each initiate and feel capable, or does one still control the choice?
- The loser wants another round. Is this playful rivalry, frustration, or simple enjoyment? Calling it loss aversion does not prove the cause.
- A limit stops repetition. Do people explore another type of interaction, quit disappointed, or wait eagerly?
- The daily reset fits a routine. Does the couple actually return, or only say they intend to?

Revise the claim when the user corrects the objective. Keep unresolved counterexamples visible. Do not treat a plausible answer from one couple as evidence for all couples or claim to know why the founders chose the rule.

## Make the claim testable

Write a narrow statement: “For [people in a situation], [rule] may lead to [observable action], because [proposed mechanism], creating [user value]. We would doubt this if [contrary result].”

Recommend the smallest comparison that isolates the disputed assumption. A one-round versus two-round cap is an example, not a default recommendation. Define whether the cap applies per game, per couple, or across the app; keep other changes out of the comparison where practical.

Specify who is included, the comparison, observation window, primary behavior, harm to watch, and the result that would change the decision. Propose thresholds explicitly if no baseline exists; never invent sample sizes, statistical confidence, or measured lift. A small qualitative pilot can expose a bad mechanism without proving retention.

For shared rituals, assess the couple as well as each partner. Choose measures tied to the claim: movement between game types, invitations and responses from each partner, both returning after the reset, or each partner's feeling at the end. Distinguish measured return from stated intent. Total minutes alone cannot establish connection or variety. Watch for pressure, unwanted nudges, frustration, and one-sided participation.

## Deliver a decision, not a feature list

Lead with the recommendation and the concrete user experience. State how many people the evidence represents and what remains unknown. Use a small scenario table when it makes the tradeoff clear, then give the revised hypothesis, strongest objection, and one next test. Explain what staying with the current rule leaves unresolved. Keep technical measurement details separate from the product summary.

Only create a shareable note or outreach draft when requested. Organizing a document, sending a message, receiving a reply, and validating a hypothesis are separate milestones. Skill invocation alone does not authorize sending messages or launching an experiment.
